17:T2d8d,My early security research largely started from structured rules. Access-control policies, security configurations, and verification conditions usually had explicit representations around which a system could reason and perform checks. As my work shifted toward data security and privacy, the problems began to change. Many requirements, data practices, and risk signals were expressed in natural language across regulations, privacy policies, terms of service, vulnerability descriptions, and news content.

My focus gradually moved toward a broader question: **how to use NLP and large language models to extract security-relevant information from complex text, build computable semantic representations, and make verifiable security and privacy judgments.**

I have mainly approached this at three levels: accurately identifying security-related meaning in text; establishing grounds for decisions through retrieval, representation learning, and structured modeling; and managing uncertainty in high-risk judgments so that results are supported by evidence and retain necessary human review.

## Starting with privacy-policy understanding

During 2023–2026, I focused more closely on privacy protection in the [data-transaction monitoring project](/en/projects/data-trading-monitoring), studying privacy-policy vagueness, data minimization, and disclosure completeness.

Privacy policies publicly describe how service providers handle data and are among the most direct textual sources for automated privacy assessment. For a model, however, the challenge is recovering the structure of the data practices described in a policy.

A practice may involve a data type, processing action, purpose, recipient, and applicability conditions. These elements may span sentences or paragraphs. The same data type can also be associated with very different purposes and compliance implications in different contexts.

I therefore began combining NLP with security assessment by dividing the problem into computable stages:

**Text understanding → Semantic structuring → Evidence retrieval → Security or compliance judgment.**

This became a recurring methodological framework in my subsequent AI for Security research.

## Modeling semantics to analyze vagueness in privacy text

[VASH](/en/research/vash) examines linguistic vagueness in privacy policies.

Traditional approaches often rely on lists of vague terms: expressions such as “information” or “service provider” are treated as signals of vagueness. Term matching alone, however, struggles with context. A general concept may be clarified later in the text, in which case judging the term in isolation overstates the vagueness of the passage.

To address this, I brought **semantic representation learning and hierarchical modeling** into privacy-text analysis.

We use semantic embeddings to identify candidate relationships among terms, then combine definitions found in privacy policies with LLM judgments to construct hypernym–hyponym relationships and a semantic hierarchy graph. On this basis, the model no longer asks only whether a word is vague; it further assesses how abstract the concept is in the current context and whether the surrounding text has already made it more specific.

We also use contextual representations and attention information from Transformers to weight the contributions of different vague expressions within a sentence, producing a more fine-grained assessment.

This work helped me think more systematically about AI's role in understanding security text: **its value lies in using contextual meaning to recover relationships among concepts, beyond matching individual keywords.**

## Combining fine-tuning, retrieval, and verification for data minimization

In another first-author paper, [How Far Are We from Automatically Identifying Violations of the Data Minimization Principle in Privacy Policies?](/en/research/data-minimization), I studied how large language models can help assess whether declared data practices satisfy the data minimization principle.

Compared with assessing linguistic vagueness, this question moves from text understanding into security and compliance judgment.

We first represent data practices as **Action–Data–Purpose** triples. LoRA fine-tuning supports extraction of the key elements, while ontologies and semantic embeddings help normalize and combine different expressions.

I then introduce dense retrieval to locate evidence in the original policy for each practice and separately verify the relationships among processing actions, data types, and purposes. This avoids relying on a single model judgment over the entire policy.

The overall pipeline is therefore closer to:

**LLMs understand and extract information; retrieval locates evidence; verification modules assess relationships; experts review high-risk and borderline cases.**

An important purpose of this design is error localization. If a judgment is incorrect, we can investigate whether the failure came from extraction, semantic combination, evidence retrieval, or relationship verification, instead of observing only an unexplained final label.

Data minimization is also strongly context-dependent. The necessity of the same data can differ across business scenarios. We therefore distinguish inherent from contextual violations, mine candidate relationships among data practices from a large collection of privacy policies, and validate those relationships through combined LLM and expert review.

This work helped establish a clear research orientation for me: **AI can substantially reduce the cost of acquiring and screening information for security analysis, while judgments involving norms, context, and risk boundaries need evidence constraints and human review to improve reliability.**

## From information extraction to evidence-based compliance judgments

As the research progressed, I became increasingly concerned with a further question: correctly understanding a passage does not guarantee a reliable final judgment.

A regulatory requirement may contain several applicability conditions and disclosure obligations. Its applicability may depend on the type of entity, data category, processing method, or user population. Establishing whether a disclosure obligation is fully met may also require evidence from a privacy policy, cookie policy, terms of service, and help center together.

My current work on privacy-policy completeness therefore frames the task as:

**Regulatory requirement understanding → Applicability assessment → Multi-document evidence retrieval → Atomic obligation verification → Evidence aggregation.**

Here, the LLM handles tasks such as decomposing requirements, generating queries, matching meanings, and performing local reasoning. Retrieval gathers candidate evidence from long documents and multiple sources. Verification checks whether each atomic requirement is sufficiently supported.

I am particularly interested in the distinction between retrieval failure and missing information. If a system does not find evidence, it cannot immediately conclude that a policy lacks the disclosure: the retriever may have missed the relevant passage.

I therefore want the system to assess:

* whether all sub-obligations have been covered;
* whether relevant evidence may remain unretrieved;
* whether descriptions across documents are consistent;
* whether the current evidence supports a definite conclusion;
* whether the model should defer judgment when evidence is insufficient.

This has gradually shifted my work from NLP classification toward **retrieval, evidence verification, multi-document reasoning, calibration, and abstention**, with a stronger emphasis on reliability.

## Extending from privacy protection to content safety

My interest in AI for Security extends beyond privacy policies.

Content safety similarly involves identifying risk signals in unstructured material and connecting model judgments to verifiable evidence. Its inputs can be more open-ended, encompassing text, images, video, and information about external events. This places additional demands on semantic understanding, cross-modal alignment, and fact checking.

ExDR, a collaboration I participated in, extends this approach to **multimodal fake-news detection and content credibility analysis**. In these tasks, the content alone may be insufficient to establish truthfulness. External knowledge and evidence are needed to examine consistency among text, images, and real-world events.

This connects closely with my privacy-compliance research. Both emphasize **acquiring evidence before making a judgment based on it**, rather than classifying content solely from its surface meaning.

Although privacy protection and content safety address different applications, they share many technical questions:

**Information extraction, semantic representation, retrieval augmentation, evidence verification, multimodal understanding, and uncertainty management.**

## How I understand AI for Security

These projects share an aim: applying AI to security tasks that have traditionally depended heavily on manual reading and expert judgment.

For me, AI for Security is not simply applying a general-purpose large model to security data; it means reorganizing model capabilities around the structure of the security task itself.

If a task requires identifying data practices, I first define a structured representation and then train a model to extract its elements. If a judgment depends on external information, I introduce retrieval rather than asking the model to rely on its parametric knowledge alone. If the conclusion carries substantial risk, evidence verification, confidence assessment, and human review become necessary.

My interest is therefore in combining:

**NLP / LLM + Retrieval + Structured Reasoning + Human Verification**

into a reliable security-analysis process.

This requires both model capabilities and a clear model of the security problem. AI output becomes meaningful when the system's objects, evidence, and decision conditions are explicit.

## From model judgments to reliable security systems

As my research has developed, the reliability of AI-based security-analysis systems has become increasingly important to me.

A plausible explanation may not be faithful to the original evidence. Several relevant retrieval results may still omit essential evidence. High confidence does not establish that a model actually knows the answer.

I am therefore currently interested in questions that span privacy protection and content safety:

How can we determine when a model needs retrieval?

How should we decide what information to retrieve?

When is the evidence sufficient to stop searching?

How can we verify that cited evidence genuinely supports a conclusion?

Can a system abstain when evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or missing?

These questions have led me further into RAG, fact checking, and agent safety.

From rule verification in access control to understanding data practices in privacy policies, and then to multi-document compliance judgments and content-safety analysis, the objects of my research have continued to change. My focus has become clearer: **using AI to automate more of security analysis while constraining its judgments through structured modeling, evidence retrieval, and verification, so that results are more reliable, explainable, and auditable.**

This is the starting point for my continuing work on [RAG and fact checking](/en/notes/rag-llm), as well as my exploration of agent safety, memory privacy, and tool-use security.18:T3202,In [privacy-text assessment](/en/notes/ai-for-security), text understanding is only the starting point. Tasks such as privacy compliance, content safety, and fact checking require a system to answer further questions: **where is the relevant evidence, is it sufficient, does it support the current conclusion, and is that conclusion faithful to the original material?**

This is an important role for RAG and fact checking in security applications. Grounding judgments in external evidence that can be located and verified offers a more reliable basis than relying solely on a model's parametric knowledge. The system must also examine evidence coverage and support relationships.

The challenge therefore extends beyond retrieving more content to building a complete **evidence chain** for a judgment.

## Retrieval addresses evidence discovery

In privacy compliance, content safety, and fact checking, relevant information is often distributed across many documents.

A privacy disclosure may span a Privacy Policy, Cookie Policy, Terms, and Help Center. Checking a news story may require its text, other reporting, event background, and images. Relying on one passage or a model's existing knowledge can leave important conditions unexamined.

RAG first helps narrow the search space within large collections of text or multimodal information.

Sparse retrieval captures explicit clues through keywords and terminology. Dense retrieval uses semantic representations to find related content expressed in different ways. A reranker further assesses the relevance of candidates to the current query.

A distinction matters here:

**Retrieval relevance does not establish evidential validity.**

A passage can be highly relevant without supporting a particular conclusion. A policy paragraph mentioning a data category establishes that the topic is present. It does not, by itself, establish that the data is used for a specific purpose or that every required disclosure has been made.

A retriever therefore returns **candidate evidence**.

## From finding text to verifying evidence

Evidence-based security analysis needs a separate verification process after retrieval.

For a candidate passage, the system should at least check:

* whether it addresses the object being assessed;
* whether the subject, action, data type, and applicability conditions match;
* whether the original text expresses support, negation, or a conditional relationship;
* whether qualifiers, exceptions, or negative statements are present;
* which part of the conclusion the passage can support.

This moves the task from semantic relevance toward **evidence entailment**.

Support, refutation, and insufficient evidence are familiar relationships in fact checking and are equally important in security analysis. In compliance assessment, a passage that appears relevant but does not entail the required fact cannot serve as valid grounds for a decision.

A more appropriate process is therefore:

**Retrieval → Evidence Verification → Decision**

in place of a simple:

**Retrieval → Generation**

Separating retrieval and judgment allows the system to examine independently whether material was found and whether that material is sufficient to support the conclusion.

## One conclusion often requires multiple pieces of evidence

Many security judgments cannot be established from a single passage.

A compliance requirement may involve an entity, data type, processing purpose, recipient, user rights, and applicability conditions. A content-safety judgment may require verifying people, times, places, events, and sources.

Evidence verification is therefore often a **multi-evidence reasoning** problem.

Multi-hop retrieval and cross-document evidence combination in fact checking offer a useful reference. A complex conclusion can be divided into smaller facts to verify, with evidence gathered and combined for each.

Compliance assessment can follow a similar approach by decomposing complex requirements into **atomic obligations**. Each obligation specifies an information need for retrieval and verification.

For example, if a requirement concerns information categories, processing purposes, and recipients, those elements may appear in different places. The overall requirement can be assessed only after all necessary components have valid support under consistent applicability conditions.

The number of pieces of evidence is therefore not the key; what matters is:

**Does the evidence cover every necessary condition on which the conclusion depends?**

## Evidence sufficiency is a separate question

Even after finding valid evidence, a system must determine whether it has enough.

This differs clearly from traditional retrieval tasks. Information retrieval typically emphasizes returning relevant results; security assessment must additionally ask:

**Is important information that could affect the final judgment still missing?**

Top-k limits the number of candidates examined; it does not establish adequate coverage of the search space. Highly relevant leading results can still omit a key condition in another document, under a different expression, or elsewhere in the context.

Evidence sufficiency must therefore be assessed against the structure of the task, including:

* which information elements the judgment requires;
* which elements already have valid evidence;
* which elements remain missing;
* which documents or sources have not yet been checked;
* whether another retrieval round continues to yield new, valid evidence.

These are central questions as RAG develops toward **evidence acquisition that accounts for coverage**.

## Not finding evidence does not establish its absence

An especially important distinction in security and compliance tasks is:

**NOT_FOUND ≠ NOT_DISCLOSED**

NOT_FOUND means that the current retrieval process has not returned relevant evidence. It describes the state of the retrieval system.

NOT_DISCLOSED is a conclusion about the object being assessed: the required information has not been found within a defined document scope and under specified checking conditions.

The first does not establish the second.

Retrieval failure can arise from inadequate query construction, biased embedding representations, unsuitable document chunking, an overly small top-k, or relevant content in a document that has not yet been searched.

Before reporting missing information, non-disclosure, or a similar conclusion, a system therefore needs to diagnose retrieval coverage.

A more reliable system should distinguish:

**Evidence being absent, evidence not yet being found, and evidence remaining unfound after a sufficiently thorough search.**

This distinction matters to privacy compliance, content moderation, and fact checking because a retrieval failure should not be misinterpreted as a problem with the object being assessed.

## Retriever, Verifier, and Aggregator

Architecturally, these tasks can be divided into three relatively independent roles.

The **Retriever** seeks potential evidence and preserves its source, location, and necessary context. It answers where relevant information might be found.

The **Verifier** assesses the relationship between candidate evidence and a specific fact or requirement: whether it supports or contradicts it, whether conditions apply, and whether the evidence is sufficiently explicit.

The **Aggregator** combines verified local results according to the original question's logical relationships and assesses whether the overall evidence is sufficient.

This separation helps locate errors.

When a final judgment is wrong, the system can investigate whether it:

**Failed to find evidence, incorrectly accepted evidence, or incorrectly aggregated evidence.**

That structure is more informative than a final label alone for security tasks that require explainable, auditable results.

## Global context and local evidence

Evidence cannot always be assessed in isolation.

In long-document and cross-document tasks, the meaning of a local passage may depend on a section heading, an earlier definition, the entity to which it applies, or a later qualification. Retrieving and verifying only a few detached sentences can easily cause semantic drift.

RAG research has therefore increasingly examined **how global context can guide local retrieval**.

One approach establishes document-level background through summaries, hierarchical indexes, or global semantic representations, then locates local evidence according to the current information need. Another issues queries dynamically based on the information gaps in a model's current judgment, adding only external information that genuinely affects the conclusion.

This is particularly clear in content safety and multimodal fact checking. A model may need to examine news reports, images, event background, and external knowledge together, beyond checking consistency within the text.

An effective retrieval system therefore does more than simply enlarge the context window; it needs to establish:

**Exactly what information is missing, and what kind of evidence is most likely to resolve the gap.**

## From fixed retrieval to adaptive RAG

Once retrieval becomes part of reasoning, it is natural to ask whether every question needs the same amount and form of retrieval.

Usually, it does not.

Some questions can be resolved using evidence already available. Others require further searches, query reformulation, or additional documents. Adaptive RAG therefore focuses on four connected decisions:

**When to retrieve → What to retrieve → Whether to continue → When to stop**

Several signals can inform these decisions.

Uncovered facts or obligations directly indicate information gaps. Verifier uncertainty can signal an unclear relationship between evidence and a claim. Contradictions among sources may warrant further searching. Repeatedly returning the same content may indicate retrieval saturation.

A single signal is rarely sufficient.

High model confidence does not establish correctness. A high retrieval score does not establish valid evidence. Repeated results may reflect a poorly constructed query.

Stopping conditions therefore need to consider several factors together:

**Coverage, evidence quality, consistency, and information gain.**

## Conclusions also need verification

The final step in an evidence chain is checking whether the generated conclusion remains faithful to the evidence.

This is the faithfulness problem familiar in fact checking and RAG.

Even when the retriever finds the right material and the verifier identifies the correct relationships, the generator can introduce information absent from the source, broaden the scope of what the evidence supports, or omit important qualifications.

A reliable system must therefore check consistency between:

**Conclusion ↔ Evidence**

This is especially important in security analysis. An error may arise from overinterpreting valid evidence rather than inventing an entirely new fact. A source may support only a possibility, a partial claim, or a statement under particular conditions, while the generated answer turns it into an unconditional conclusion.

Reliability thus depends on checking not only whether evidence supports a conclusion, but also whether:

**The conclusion stays within the semantic scope of what the evidence permits.**

## The role of RAG and fact checking in security

Privacy compliance and content safety have different objectives, yet they share a similar technical structure:

**Understand the question → Identify information needs → Retrieve evidence → Verify evidence → Assess sufficiency → Aggregate evidence → Verify the conclusion.**

RAG's role here is not merely to extend the model's knowledge but to connect the model to external facts.

Fact checking provides ways to assess whether that connection is reliable: whether evidence is relevant, supportive, and complete, and whether the final conclusion is faithful to it.

Rather than pursuing stronger generation capabilities alone, it is more worthwhile to focus on how to build **traceable evidence chains**.

For privacy compliance, this determines whether a disclosure assessment has sufficient grounds. For content safety, it determines whether a risk judgment can be checked against external facts. For agents and tool use, it also concerns the information on which a model acts and when it should stop, search further, or abstain.

## Starting with access-control models

During 2019–2021, I participated in a [project on authorization for heterogeneous data](/en/projects/heterogeneous-data-authorization). My work focused on fine-grained data authorization, access-control policy management, and integration with big-data platforms, including system implementation in Hadoop and Huawei FusionInsight HD environments. My undergraduate thesis also concerned fine-grained HDFS access control based on CoAC.

The central question at this stage was concrete: **how to translate real authorization requirements into access-control policies that a system can execute.**

Access control describes relationships among subjects, resources, operations, and constraints. An authorization requirement might specify that a class of users may read a class of data only when particular attribute conditions hold. To execute that rule, a system needs to establish how subjects and resources are represented, how attributes are matched, how conditions are combined, and how overlapping rules determine the final authorization result.

An access-control model therefore does more than store permissions. It provides a formal representation that accurately expresses authorization semantics and supports execution and verification.

This work gave me an early understanding of security modeling: first identify the objects, constraints, and relationships involved in a security problem, then consider how to turn them into a machine-processable representation.

## From policy execution to policy analysis

My work subsequently expanded from executing individual policies to **generating, migrating, and verifying policy sets**.

In the [Huawei collaboration](/en/projects/industry-access-control), I participated in policy migration, policy mining, and policy conflict detection. These tasks placed greater emphasis on relationships among rules and on whether policies preserved their original security semantics after translation or reconstruction.

### Policy migration: preserving authorization semantics across representations

Access-control systems often use different policy models and forms of expression. Policy migration converts source-system rules into policies that a target system can execute, while preserving the original authorization semantics as closely as possible.

Migration therefore goes beyond format conversion. The key question is whether **the subjects, resources, permissions, and constraints expressed in the source policy retain equivalent meanings in the target model**.

This work drew my attention to semantic correspondence between rule representations. A syntactically valid translation does not necessarily preserve the intended security meaning.

### Policy mining: recovering rules from existing authorizations

Policy mining addresses a different question: given many subjects, resources, and authorization relationships, how can we infer a concise, interpretable set of access-control rules?

Candidate rules can be evaluated through **their coverage of existing authorizations and their complexity**, while also considering correctness and simplicity. A submodular objective captures diminishing marginal returns as rules are added. This allows efficient approximation methods to balance coverage of existing authorizations against the size and complexity of the resulting policy set.

The value of this formulation is that it turns policy quality into an optimization problem. The aim is to cover sufficiently many valid authorizations with a limited number of clear rules that administrators can understand and maintain.

### Conflict detection: analyzing the behavior of a policy set

When many policies coexist, the correctness of individual rules does not guarantee consistent behavior across the policy set. Rules may overlap in their subjects, conditions, or authorization outcomes, creating potential conflicts.

Policy conflict detection therefore asks whether **multiple rules acting together can produce inconsistent or unintended authorization outcomes**.

Across migration, mining, and conflict detection, my focus expanded from executing a rule to analyzing the overall behavior of a rule set. I increasingly came to view security problems in terms of rule modeling, semantic transformation, and constraint-based reasoning.

## From structured rules to natural-language security specifications

In subsequent work on data compliance and security configurations, I encountered another change: many security rules first appear in natural-language documents rather than in executable form.

I participated in parsing and verifying CIS security configuration baselines. CIS Benchmark requirements are generally described in natural language, while actual system states are expressed through configuration settings. Automated verification first requires identifying the object to check, the configuration location, the expected value, and the relevant constraints, then converting them into structured checking rules.

The task can therefore be understood as:

**Extract rules from natural-language security documents, then use those rules to determine whether system configurations meet the requirements.**

Unlike the earlier access-control work, the rules are not given in advance. They must first be recovered through text understanding.

This change made me more aware of how language understanding affects security verification. The result depends both on the checking logic and on whether the preceding step accurately interpreted the specification. If rule extraction omits a condition, an object, or a scope restriction, even a correctly executed verification procedure may answer the wrong question.

Text understanding thus became a central part of the security-verification process.

## From security specifications to vulnerability descriptions

A similar problem arises in vulnerability research.

Logical attack graphs require structured interaction rules describing the preconditions of an attack and the system-state changes it produces. These rules allow an attack graph to infer relationships among vulnerabilities and attack steps and construct multi-stage attack paths.

Much vulnerability knowledge, however, first appears in natural-language sources such as CVE descriptions and vulnerability reports. Attack-graph construction therefore depends on another important step:

**How can natural-language vulnerability descriptions be converted into attack rules that support automated reasoning?**

[INTERNIST](/en/research/internist), a later collaboration I participated in, addresses this question.

INTERNIST defines a general predicate system based on cybersecurity states and divides the construction of logical attack rules from vulnerability descriptions into four stages:

**Predicate extraction → Parameter association → Parameter filling → Causal organization.**

Predicates represent the system states and behaviors involved in an attack. Parameter association identifies entities shared across predicates. Parameter filling supplies information about software, hosts, privileges, and network states. Causal organization then connects preconditions to outcomes according to the exploitation process.

Compared with having a model generate a complex rule end-to-end, this staged formulation explicitly constrains rule structure and reduces the difficulty of completing complex logical reasoning in a single step.

For training, the method uses LoRA multi-task fine-tuning so that rule-construction subtasks share underlying semantic representations. Curriculum learning and Focal Loss help the model progressively learn complex examples and place greater emphasis on difficult, low-confidence examples.

To address missing background information in vulnerability descriptions, INTERNIST also introduces external knowledge about product functions and operating mechanisms. Few-shot prompting helps handle information gaps and semantic ambiguity.

The work reinforced an important point for me: **natural-language security text and formal security reasoning need a stable, verifiable intermediate representation.**

## From extracting rules to making judgments from text

Looking back, the objects of my research have gradually changed.

In early access-control research, rules were usually already structured. My focus was on representing, executing, migrating, and verifying them.

In CIS baseline verification and vulnerability-rule construction, rules were embedded in natural-language documents. The process therefore became:

> Natural-language documents → Structured security rules → System-level judgments

Privacy-policy and data-compliance research introduced a further change. Requirements often come from laws and regulatory documents, while the objects being assessed are themselves natural-language documents such as privacy policies and terms of service. The research question becomes:

> Natural-language requirements → Natural-language evidence → Compliance judgments

The scope has thus expanded from extracting rules from text to assess a system, toward establishing evidential relationships across documents and making judgments from them.

The technical methods have expanded accordingly: from access-control models, rule optimization, and constraint reasoning to information extraction, text representations, retrieval, large language models, and fact checking.

The central question remains: **how to turn complex security requirements into explicit grounds for a decision, while keeping the resulting conclusion traceable, explainable, and verifiable.**

I think AI is a tool for improving efficiency. It lets us focus more on implementing and testing our own ideas. But it also asks more of our taste, whether we are doing research or building products.

Research requires academic taste: producing work that is simple, effective, and elegant, like Kaiming He's. Here is a [Chinese-language post about Kaiming He and research taste](https://www.xiaohongshu.com/discovery/item/6a840aff0000000005028165?source=webshare&xhsshare=pc_web&xsec_token=ABbEXSdoif03AH48Ju-V4MZL9c0tfCosZlZ_meD72xxdg=&xsec_source=pc_share) if you are interested. Building products also takes good taste if we want to waste fewer resources and tokens and make something meaningful that people enjoy using.

I also think we still need a foundation in programming and mathematics. We should understand the principles we need for our work. AI can help with this too, by helping us learn the relevant knowledge quickly or apply it when we need it.

Before using AI, I think it is essential to break down the task and clarify its boundaries. During the work, we also need to think about where the human belongs in human-in-the-loop. Getting this right helps AI save us time and reduces the amount of repair work we have to do later.

## Tools and models I use

The tools I use regularly are Cursor, Codex, and WorkBuddy. The models I use include GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Terra, GPT-5.6 Luna, Claude-sonnet-5, Claude-opus-5, and Deepseek-v4.

I choose different models and working modes depending on the task. I mainly use models with strong reasoning capabilities, such as GPT-5.6 Sol, with Plan mode for discussing approaches, reviewing related work, designing prompts, and testing concepts. For engineering implementation, I mainly use models with strong coding capabilities, such as GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Terra, and GPT-5.6 Luna, with Target mode.

## Building RAGForge with AI

RAGForge is a modular RAG experimentation framework I have gradually built during my research. It started because I needed to try and compare different RAG approaches frequently. These usually involve data processing, text chunking, retrieval, reranking, generation, and evaluation. Implementing each approach separately meant a lot of repeated work and made it difficult to keep experiment configurations and evaluation methods consistent. So I abstracted the shared stages and, with Codex's help, built a framework whose components can be freely combined to quickly assemble and compare different RAG pipelines.

![RAGForge template page showing a pipeline graph and its configuration panel.](/assets/posts/working-with-ai/ragforge-templates.png)

*RAGForge's pipeline templates and editor.*

I was responsible for defining the framework's scope, the component-registration coding style, the core module abstractions, and the interfaces between modules. RAGForge uses a typed DAG to describe a pipeline, with retrievers, rerankers, generators, and evaluators designed as pluggable components. A compiler checks node references, input and output types, and topology. A runner handles dataset execution, caching, traces, and metric aggregation. The frontend uses React and ReactFlow for drag-and-drop pipeline composition, configuration editing, and viewing experiment results.

![RAGForge component library showing component descriptions and input and output types.](/assets/posts/working-with-ai/ragforge-components.png)

*The component library shows what each component does and its inputs and outputs.*

AI mainly acts as my development collaborator in this project. I first define a module's goal, inputs and outputs, and interactions with other modules. Then I ask Codex to understand the repository and carry out the implementation, changes across files, refactoring, testing, and debugging. I review and iterate based on the test results and the goals of the experiment.

For example, when checking early experiment code, Codex found overly permissive evaluation conditions for multiple-choice questions and baseline text truncation that could affect fair comparisons. I would then determine whether an issue was an implementation bug or a design problem and decide how to change it.

![RAGForge experiment run page with configuration, pre-run checks, and a task queue.](/assets/posts/working-with-ai/ragforge-run.png)

*The run page is where I check configurations, submit tasks, and view progress.*

RAGForge currently supports eight classes of experimental pipelines, including BM25, Dense, Hybrid, and Graph. It has been used in multiple RAG cases to compare approaches within the same framework. The user manual below covers data, components, pipeline design, experiment execution, and results management.

![RAGForge user manual with a table of contents and Chinese and English language options.](/assets/posts/working-with-ai/ragforge-manual.png)

*The RAGForge user manual.*

This project has helped me settle into a fairly consistent way of working with AI. I handle problem definition, architecture, module breakdown, interface design, and acceptance. AI speeds up implementation, refactoring, and testing, and broadens the scope of code inspection. I make the final technical decisions and check the experimental conclusions so that the code stays on track as it grows.1b:T4b5,When a research direction changes, what remains is often not a particular model or dataset, but a way of judging the problem.

## The shared question

From access control to privacy-policy compliance, the work repeatedly involves three actions: understanding rules, locating evidence, and deciding whether that evidence supports a conclusion. RAG and fact-checking bring the same actions into large-language-model systems.

## From rules to evidence

Privacy regulations are not merely keywords to match. Applicability conditions, atomic obligations, and concrete disclosures in a document need to be distinguished. Reliable retrieval likewise asks for more than a semantically similar passage: the evidence must actually answer the question.

## What remains open

One current question is whether a system can distinguish “the document did not disclose it” from “retrieval failed to find the disclosure.” This distinction matters both to trustworthy compliance assessment and to deciding when a model should continue retrieving or stop.
