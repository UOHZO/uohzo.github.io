17:T482a,> **Core question:** How can we automatically assess whether the data practices declared in privacy policies violate the data minimization principle? We propose DataMini, a Human–LLM collaborative evaluation framework for privacy policies at scale.

## Overview

DataMini represents data practices as **Action × Data × Purpose** triples and divides the abstract minimization problem into **inherent violations** and **contextual violations**. It mines frequent associations from large-scale privacy policies, validates them with LLMs and experts to create reusable **compliance mappings**, automatically filters clearly compliant practices, and subjects only suspected violations to Human–LLM review.

Data practice extraction achieves **83.46% Accuracy**. End-to-end violation identification achieves **Precision 0.9717, Recall 0.7063, and F1 0.8180**, while reducing the information experts need to read by approximately **80%**.

## Why undertake this work?

This work originated in data compliance projects I participated in and was further developed through the project on key technologies for monitoring the entire data transaction lifecycle. These projects examine whether data is handled compliantly during collection, use, sharing, derivation, and storage. Both the Personal Information Protection Law and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) impose data minimization requirements: personal information processing should be directly related to its purpose and limited to what is necessary to achieve it. This led me to ask: **are service providers' declared data practices actually relevant and necessary for their service purposes?**

Privacy policies are a natural assessment target: they are **legally effective documents** that service providers give users to describe data collection, use, sharing, and other practices. They disclose:

- What operation is performed on data: **Action**.
- What personal information is processed: **Data**.
- Why the information is processed: **Purpose**.

I therefore formalize a practice as `Data Practice = (Action, Data, Purpose)`. For example, `(use, interests, advertising)` means using information about a user's interests for advertising.

> This work assesses **whether practices declared in privacy policies comply with data minimization**. It does not verify whether a company follows its policy at runtime. Assessing actual operational compliance requires evidence such as program analysis, network traffic, or logs.

## Why is automation needed?

Privacy policies are often long, technical, and structurally complex. Ordinary users find it difficult and are unlikely to spend substantial time judging every practice's necessity. Relying entirely on privacy experts for large-scale review is costly. My goal is therefore to build:

> **A fine-grained, scalable, traceable automated approach to data minimization assessment that reduces expert review effort.**

## What are the problems with existing methods?

Existing data minimization assessment methods broadly fall into two categories.

### 1 Enumerating necessary data practices using expert knowledge

Experts predefine necessary practices, and practices outside that set are treated as potential violations. This faces three problems:

1. **Data minimization is context-dependent.** The same data may have very different necessity across application types and purposes.
2. Application functions keep changing, making it difficult to **enumerate every reasonable practice**.
3. Expert enumeration does not scale well to a large, rapidly changing application ecosystem.

### 2 Comparing similar applications

Another approach finds functionally similar applications, measures which data they commonly collect, and treats frequent practices as necessary. This makes strong assumptions:

- Data commonly collected by peer applications is necessary.
- The collected data reasonably serves the stated purposes.
- Enough suitable comparison applications can always be found.

These assumptions do not always hold. Existing methods also often focus only on collection, although minimization applies more broadly to operations such as use, storage, and sharing.

### 3 Existing data practice extraction is too coarse

Tools such as PolicyLint, PurPliance, and PoliGraph extract some practices using rules, syntactic patterns, or coarse categories. For example, `collect email address` may become `collect personal information`, incorrectly broadening the original meaning. Losing this granularity directly affects subsequent judgments about whether the data is necessary.

### 4 Direct LLM-as-a-Judge raises ethical concerns in compliance assessment

A straightforward approach is to give the entire policy to an LLM and ask it to identify noncompliant practices end to end. However:

- Long policies introduce long-range dependencies and context forgetting.
- LLMs may hallucinate judgments without clear textual evidence.
- Minimization involves subjective and contextual judgments, so model decisions can diverge from expert decisions.
- In compliance settings, relying entirely on automated models for final decisions conflicts with ethical requirements.

My approach therefore combines **automatic screening, LLM assistance, and final expert confirmation**.

## What are the challenges?

### Challenge 1: Minimization is difficult to quantify directly

Regulations require processing to be relevant and necessary, but do not list permitted data for every application and function. We must first define what constitutes a data minimization violation. This requires translating a legal principle into a computable assessment structure, beyond ordinary text classification. Before our method, prior work had not provided a satisfactory definition and quantification approach; one of the OpenReview comments we received also reflects this gap.

![OpenReview comment](/assets/research/data-minimization/openreview.png)

### Challenge 2: Necessity is strongly context-dependent

The same practice can be reasonable in one application and questionable in another. For example, `(collect, financial records, functionality)` may be necessary for a financial application but problematic for an unrelated application. Assessment must consider **context such as application type**, in addition to the semantic relationship between data and purpose.

### Challenge 3: Privacy policies are long, complex, and unstructured

Assessment depends on accurate triples. Errors in Action, Data, or Purpose extraction undermine subsequent compliance judgments. During extraction, I encountered:

- Numerous synonymous expressions.
- Multiple actions, data types, and purposes within one paragraph.
- Relationships that span more than one sentence.
- Long-context limitations and hallucinations in direct LLM extraction.

The challenge is therefore **fine-grained extraction, normalization, and relationship verification**.

### Challenge 4: Frequency does not establish compliance

Learning common patterns can reduce expert enumeration costs, but frequent real-world behavior may itself violate minimization. Statistical regularities can produce **candidate knowledge**, which still requires validation. DataMini therefore uses both LLMs and experts to verify candidate mappings.

### Challenge 5: Accuracy conflicts with manual effort

Fully manual review is the most reliable but does not scale. Full automation is efficient but insufficiently reliable for decisions involving law and ethics. The central question is:

> **Which parts can be safely automated, and which should remain with people?**

## Core innovations and contributions

### 1 Fine-grained modeling of data minimization violations

Based on **data–purpose relevance**, we distinguish two levels:

1. **Inherent violation:** Is an **Action × Data** combination reasonable for its **Purpose**? This can be assessed without knowing the application type. A clear lack of a necessary relationship between the processed data and the purpose can indicate an inherent violation. The corresponding check is `Purpose → Action × Data`.
2. **Contextual violation:** Is the **Data / Purpose** reasonable for the **App Type**? A practice may appear reasonable in isolation but inappropriate in a particular application context. This requires checking `App Type → Purpose` and `App Type → Data`.

This contribution addresses **how the problem is defined and made computable**.

### 2 An Extract–Combine–Verify data practice extraction pipeline

The pipeline follows `LoRA-fine-tuned extraction → Ontology normalization and combination → Two rounds of retrieval-based verification`, converting complex policies into fine-grained `(Action, Data, Purpose)` triples.

This design addresses semantic granularity, synonym normalization, combinatorial explosion, and whether triples are actually supported by the source text.

### 3 A reusable compliance baseline from large-scale real privacy policies

We extract **875,499 triples from 12,519 policies**, mine frequent candidates using PMI and co-occurrence probability, and validate them with LLMs and experts. The resulting **5,130 compliance mappings** turn expert knowledge into a **reusable, scalable compliance baseline**.

### 4 Human–LLM collaboration beyond simple LLM-as-a-Judge

Compliance mappings filter clearly compliant practices. Practices that do not fully match become suspected violations for LLM analysis and final expert confirmation.

Expert work shifts from `Read the entire policy → Find problems → Analyze them` to `Review selected suspected problems and brief rationales → Verify`. Experiments show approximately **80%** less information for experts to process.

## Method

### 1 Overall framework

DataMini has two parts:

![DataMini compliance baseline construction and Human–LLM evaluation framework](/assets/research/data-minimization/overview.png)

1. **Compliance Baseline Construction:** Build reusable compliance knowledge from large-scale privacy policies.
2. **Human–LLM Collaborative Evaluation:** Apply the baseline to new policies, automatically filter clearly compliant practices, and send suspected violations for further LLM and expert review.

The central principle is:

> **Machines handle large volumes of repetitive, relatively clear judgments, leaving experts to focus on difficult boundary cases.**

### 2 Detailed method

### 2.1 Representing data practices

Each practice is represented as `(Action, Data, Purpose)`. Three ontologies standardize expressions across policies: the Action ontology contains 51 action categories; the Purpose ontology contains 56 processing purposes; and the Data ontology extends OPI with 266 data terms. Google Play's 48 application categories represent application context.

- **Inherent violations** can be detected from relationships among Action, Data, and Purpose without a specific application type.
- **Contextual violations** require the application category: the same practice can be reasonable in one type of application and unreasonable in another.

These correspond to three relationships: `Purpose → Action × Data`, `App Type → Purpose`, and `App Type → Data`.

### 2.2 Compliance baseline construction

DataMini first constructs reusable compliance mappings from a large corpus of policies.

#### 2.2.1 Dataset construction

Starting with 36,167 Google Play package names, we collect application categories, titles, privacy policy URLs, and other metadata, then crawl the policies. Filtering invalid packages, inaccessible links, and non-English policies yields 12,519 policies with corresponding application metadata. The corpus provides sufficient scale and category diversity for estimating practice co-occurrence.

#### 2.2.2 Data practice extraction

We design a three-stage pipeline: **Extract → Combine → Verify**.

![Data practice extraction pipeline](/assets/research/data-minimization/pipeline.png)

**(1) Extract:** Clean policy webpages into Markdown while preserving document structure. A LoRA-fine-tuned LLM separately extracts Action, Data, and Purpose paragraph by paragraph. Paragraph-level extraction retains local context while avoiding processing an entire long document. Extracting elements separately also reduces omissions and incorrect associations when multiple elements coexist in a paragraph.

**(2) Combine:** Normalize synonymous expressions such as `surname` and `last name`. Embedding similarity maps raw elements into standardized Action, Data, and Purpose ontology terms. Then construct candidate `Action × Data` pairs, avoiding a full Cartesian product of all three elements.

**(3) Verify:** Retrieve source evidence to check candidate relationships. BGE-M3 uses structure-aware chunks that prioritize headings, sections, and paragraphs. The first verification round asks whether an Action–Data pair actually exists. For confirmed pairs, the second identifies the corresponding purposes, producing complete triples. Retrieval grounds each judgment in the policy's own evidence. The two rounds also avoid the combinatorial explosion of directly combining every Action, Data, and Purpose.

#### 2.2.3 Mining frequent mappings

Extraction from 12,519 policies produces 875,499 triples. We mine three candidate mapping types: `Purpose → Action × Data`, `App Type → Purpose`, and `App Type → Data`. They capture action–data combinations common for a purpose, purposes common for an application type, and data commonly processed by that type.

PMI first filters weak data–purpose associations; co-occurrence probability then removes infrequent, incidental relationships. **Frequency generates candidates but does not prove compliance**, since unreasonable practices can also be widespread.

#### 2.2.4 Validating compliance mappings

Three different LLMs independently judge whether each candidate mapping satisfies data minimization, returning Y/N decisions and rationales. Majority voting produces preliminary consensus and confidence. Domain experts then consider the decisions and rationales to make the final determination, yielding reusable compliance mappings.

---

### 2.3 Human–LLM collaborative evaluation

Evaluation of a new application has three steps.

**(1) Preprocessing:** Collect its policy, category, description, and other metadata, then extract practices with Extract–Combine–Verify.

**(2) Mapping matching:** Compare each practice with all three mapping types. A mismatch in `Purpose → Action × Data` indicates a **potential inherent violation**. A mismatch in `App Type → Purpose` or `App Type → Data` indicates a **potential contextual violation**. A mismatch flags a practice for review; it does not itself establish a violation.

**(3) Review:** Give suspected violations, application metadata, and the policy to an LLM. It assesses whether a violation exists, identifies its type, and provides a rationale. Domain experts make the final confirmation.

## Experiments

Experiments examine three questions:

- Can data practices be extracted accurately?
- Is the constructed compliance baseline reliable?
- Can DataMini effectively identify data minimization violations?

### (1) Data practice extraction performance

**Baselines:** Compare six open-source LLMs—Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, Gemma-3-12B, Baichuan2-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Baichuan2-13B—before and after fine-tuning, alongside leading extraction tools PurPliance, PolicyLint, and PoliGraph.

**Metrics:** Element extraction uses Exact Match F1 and ROUGE-L F1. Complete triples also use embedding-based semantic similarity, averaging similarities across Action, Data, and Purpose to define:

- **Coverage:** How well predicted triples cover reference triples.
- **Accuracy:** How closely predicted triples agree with reference triples.

**Implementation:** LoRA rank=8, scaling factor=16, and dropout=0.05; training uses one NVIDIA A100-SXM4 40GB GPU.

**Results:** Fine-tuning substantially improves all models. ROUGE-L F1 increases by approximately `0.17–0.57` and Exact Match F1 by `0.17–0.58` over base models. Fine-tuned LLMs also substantially outperform PurPliance, PolicyLint, and PoliGraph overall. **Fine-tuned Gemma-3-12B** performs best overall and is selected for subsequent extraction, producing 875,499 triples from all 12,519 policies.

![Data practice extraction performance of LLMs and existing tools](/assets/research/data-minimization/lora.png)

This experiment establishes the reliability of the structured inputs used for compliance assessment: inaccurate practice extraction would undermine subsequent violation identification.

### (2) Reliability of the compliance baseline

Two metrics are examined:

- **Violation Rate:** The proportion of candidate mappings that do not satisfy data minimization.
- **Candidate Set Size:** The number retained at a given threshold.

Low thresholds retain excessive noise and increase expert review costs; high thresholds may discard useful patterns. The tradeoff is between candidate quality, coverage, and manual verification cost.

### (3) PMI and co-occurrence probability thresholds

A key finding is that **high co-occurrence can still correspond to violations**. Broad relationships such as `personal information → service improvement` occur frequently and have strong statistical associations, yet can remain unreasonable under data minimization. LLM and expert validation therefore remain necessary.

### (4) Violation identification

Preliminary violation labels from compliance mappings achieve **82.76% Accuracy** against expert ground truth, supporting the baseline's usefulness for initial screening and violation-type classification.

End-to-end identification achieves **Precision 0.9717, Recall 0.7063, and F1 0.8180**. High precision indicates relatively cautious violation judgments. Recall of 0.7063 also shows that some real violations remain undetected, reflecting the complexity and subjectivity of minimization. Some cases require jointly considering application context, functional meaning, data sensitivity, and legal requirements, and model judgments can still diverge from experts. This motivates the final human-in-the-loop design.

### (5) Manual review effort

Beyond identification performance, the paper measures how much information experts need to read.
