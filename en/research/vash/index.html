Abstract

A semantic hierarchy graph quantifies term vagueness, while contextual detection corrects pseudo-vagueness.

Full research process

Core question: How can we automatically assess linguistic vagueness in privacy policies at a fine-grained, interpretable level? VASH constructs a semantic hierarchy graph for the privacy domain, distinguishes degrees of term vagueness, identifies contextual explanations that mitigate apparent or “false” vagueness, and produces a policy-level vagueness score.

Privacy policies are legal documents through which service providers disclose data processing practices. Data protection regulations such as the GDPR require clear wording. In practice, companies may use general language to accommodate changing technologies and business environments, or vague expressions that obscure users' understanding of processing. Measuring vagueness is therefore an important part of assessing privacy policy compliance.

Existing approaches mainly use neural networks or term-based methods. Neural methods directly predict vagueness but have limited interpretability and lack sufficient high-quality annotations. Term-based methods are more interpretable, yet often treat different terms as equally vague and struggle with domain-specific vocabulary and expressions already clarified by context.

We propose semantic hierarchy graph-based assessment. Hypernym–hyponym relationships among privacy terms provide fine-grained, interpretable term scores. Cue words and the graph identify false vagueness and adjust those scores. Attention weighting then produces the final policy score.

We construct two datasets spanning 475 industries: TermEx, with 37,122 latest-version policies for term extraction, and PriPolicy, with 75,145 policies from 2016–2025 for large-scale analysis. On PriPolicy, VASH identifies an average of 100.21 vagueness-related terms per policy, achieves 0.82 F1 for false vagueness detection, and shows strong agreement with human judgments.

Further analysis finds that policies have become longer and contain more explanations over the past decade, with little improvement in clarity. Company size has no significant effect on vagueness, whereas industry differences are substantial. Policy updates do not guarantee clearer wording: the quality of additions matters more than their quantity.

This work was also project-driven, originating in my earlier data compliance projects and further developed through the project on key technologies for monitoring the entire data transaction lifecycle. These projects address compliance during data collection, use, and sharing. Privacy policies publicly describe service providers' processing practices and have legal effect, making them suitable objects for automated compliance and privacy quality assessment.

Data protection laws and regulations, including the Personal Information Protection Law and GDPR, emphasize clear policy wording. Policies must cover complex, evolving practices, which can encourage general expressions. Excessive vagueness, however, undermines users' understanding and can conceal the actual scope of processing.

The resulting question is:

Can we quantify vague expressions in privacy policies and distinguish genuine vagueness from expressions already clarified by context?

Existing methods face three main problems:

  • Coarse treatment of differences between terms. Counting vague words or vague sentences effectively assigns the same vagueness to different terms. Yet information and email address occupy different levels of abstraction: the former covers a broader scope and is more vague.
  • Insufficient privacy-domain vocabulary coverage. Existing ontologies have limited scope, while policies span many industries with specialized terms. Missing vocabulary undermines identification and scoring.
  • Inadequate handling of false vagueness. A general term becomes less vague when the policy supplies specific examples or explanations. Treating it as an ordinary vague term overestimates the sentence's vagueness.

Challenge 1: Vagueness is hierarchical rather than binary

A broad term such as information and a specific term such as last name have different degrees of vagueness. We need a representation of semantic hierarchy and a way to convert its structure into continuous vagueness scores.

Challenge 2: Covering privacy-domain terms across industries

Existing ontologies omit many terms found in real policies. Direct corpus extraction, however, introduces spelling variants, synonyms, rare terms, and redundancy. The challenge combines large-scale term discovery, cleaning, normalization, and organization of semantic relationships.

Challenge 3: Term relationships depend on domain context

A word can have different meanings in ordinary language and privacy policies. Predicting hypernym, hyponym, or synonym relationships from surface wording alone can be misleading. Relation prediction therefore needs definitional context from privacy policies.

Challenge 4: Context can resolve vagueness

Information is broad, but in “information, such as identity information and geographic location information,” the specific terms explain the general concept. We must identify that relationship and incorporate it into the scoring formula, rather than retain the original high score or merely add a flag.

1 A semantic hierarchy graph for fine-grained term vagueness

Hypernym–hyponym relationships organize terms into a DAG from general to specific concepts. Reachable source and sink nodes, path depths, and other topological features determine term vagueness scores.

2 A privacy policy vocabulary spanning 475 industries

Terms extracted from real policies are aligned with and supplemented by existing privacy ontologies. The resulting PP-Lexicon contains 8,229 terms. It supports a semantic hierarchy DAG with 7,453 nodes and 11,318 edges, and a synonym dictionary with 1,243 terms, including 467 central terms.

3 RAG for semantic relationship prediction

GPT-4o classifies candidate pairs as hypernym, hyponym, synonym, or unrelated. To ground these decisions in domain context, the system retrieves relevant definitions for both terms from a policy-derived vector database and provides them with the task specification.

4 Incorporating false vagueness into final scores

Cue words and SHGraph identify explanations within and across sentences. When specific terms explain a general term, VASH lowers the explained term's score before sentence scoring. False vagueness detection is thus part of quantification itself.

5 A large-scale longitudinal, cross-industry dataset and empirical findings

The paper constructs 75,145 privacy policies spanning 475 industries and 2016–2025, then analyzes vagueness across time, company sizes, and industries.

1 Overall framework

VASH comprises three modules:

Module 1: Dataset construction → Module 2: Semantic hierarchy graph generation → Module 3: Vagueness quantification

First, construct TermEx and PriPolicy. Next, extract privacy-domain terms from TermEx, predict semantic relationships, and build SHGraph. Finally, use SHGraph to detect false vagueness and compute term, sentence, and policy-level scores.

2 Dataset construction

The two datasets serve different purposes:

  • TermEx: 37,122 latest-version policies across 475 industries, for term extraction and SHGraph construction.
  • PriPolicy: 75,145 policies across the same 475 industries, including historical versions from 2016–2025, for evaluation and analysis across time, industries, and company sizes.

Construction has four steps:

  1. Inner-join Privaseer metadata with the Free Company Dataset on their shared domain field to add industry information.
  2. Use stratified sampling by industry to select policy URLs for TermEx and PriPolicy.
  3. Obtain historical snapshots through the Wayback Machine.
  4. Extract policy text from HTML snapshots.

This addresses limited industry coverage and insufficient post-2020 data in existing datasets, enabling longitudinal analysis of clarity alongside regulatory changes.

3 Semantic hierarchy graph generation

This module represents relationships from abstract to specific terms and assigns term-level vagueness scores through four steps:

Privacy policy lexicon generation → Candidate term pair generation → Semantic relationship prediction → Term vagueness calculation

3.1 Privacy policy lexicon generation

Extract candidate terms from TermEx using 30 part-of-speech patterns. For example, NN NN captures consecutive nouns such as email address. Apply spelling correction and normalization, then remove infrequent or redundant terms.

Approximately 20,000 candidates remain for manual review. Alignment with and supplementation from existing privacy ontologies yields 8,229 terms in PP-Lexicon.

Why this design? Corpus extraction expands the limited coverage of existing ontologies, while manual cleaning and ontology alignment control noise.

3.2 Candidate term pair generation

Predicting relationships for every pair among 8,229 terms would be expensive. We first identify pairs likely to have semantic relationships.

Synonym replacement: Compute cosine similarity between term embeddings. Manually inspect pairs above 0.9 similarity to confirm synonyms, group them into disjoint sets, and select the term with the greatest cumulative similarity to the others as each set's central term.

Transitive-closure-based candidate generation: Establish direct associations at cosine similarity ≥ 0.7, then iteratively expand each term's associated set using transitive closure. Only these likely related pairs proceed to prediction.

Why this design? Embeddings and graph connectivity narrow the search space, concentrating costly LLM judgments on more promising pairs.

3.3 Semantic relationship prediction

GPT-4o assigns one of four labels to each candidate pair:

  • 1: Term 1 is a hypernym of term 2.
  • -1: Term 1 is a hyponym of term 2.
  • 0: The terms are synonyms.
  • 2: None of these semantic relationships applies.

VASH combines RAG with a structured prompt containing Context, Task, and Action.

The system extracts definitional text from policies and builds a vector database. For each pair, it retrieves the most relevant definition chunk for each term and provides the definitions, relationship task, and output format to GPT-4o.

Why RAG? Definitions ground predictions in the terms' actual privacy-policy meanings, enabling domain-context-based semantic judgments.

The resulting synonym dictionary contains 1,243 terms with 467 central terms, and the DAG contains 7,453 nodes and 11,318 edges.

3.4 Term vagueness calculation

SHGraph is formalized as a DAG, SHGraph = (V, E, f). Nodes correspond to PP-Lexicon terms, edges point from hypernyms to hyponyms, and f(v) ∈ [0,100] gives each term's vagueness, with higher values indicating greater vagueness.

Nodes fall into three categories:

  • Source nodes: No incoming edges and at least one outgoing edge; general concepts such as information.
  • Sink nodes: Incoming edges but no outgoing edges; specific concepts such as last name.
  • Intermediate nodes: Both incoming and outgoing edges.

The paper defines reachable sources, reachable sinks, and semantic chains. A chain runs from a source to a sink, with vagueness decreasing along it, as in information → ... → email address.

The intuition is:

  • More reachable sinks and greater depth indicate that a term encompasses more specific concepts and is more vague.
  • More reachable sources impose more superordinate semantic constraints, reducing vagueness.

VASH combines source-side and sink-side topology:

f(v) = α · fs(v) + (1 − α) · ft(v)

Both fs(v) and ft(v) consider reachable-node counts and longest-path distances. Clipped normalization controls extreme values, and a square-root transform reduces outliers' marginal influence. The weight α varies with connectivity above and below the node.

Four parameters—norm_s, norm_t, φ_s, and φ_t—are selected by analyzing structural distributions and performing a heuristic search to:

  1. Keep vagueness as monotonically decreasing as possible along semantic chains.
  2. Minimize the coefficient of variation.

Why calculate scores from graph structure instead of asking an LLM to assign them? Graph structure explicitly grounds scores in a term's position in the hierarchy, providing stable, interpretable, reusable term-level measurements.

4 Vagueness quantification

4.1 Identifying false vagueness

The paper calls cases where specific terms explain a general term false vagueness. This does not mean the sentence has no vagueness; it means that using the general term's original score would overestimate vagueness in that context.

Cue words such as such as identify candidate explanatory sentences. The 30 POS patterns extract terms, and SHGraph verifies hypernym–hyponym relationships between general and specific terms.

VASH handles both:

  • Intra-sentence false vagueness: The explanation occurs within the same sentence.
  • Inter-sentence false vagueness: The explanation spans adjacent or related sentences.

Confirmed explanations reduce the explained term's contribution to the current sentence's vagueness.

4.2 Quantifying Vagueness of Sentences

VASH uses attention weights from BERT's final layer to measure each term's contextual importance, yielding:

Vag(s) = Σ(ω_t · f(t)) / Σω_t

  • f(t): The term's vagueness score in SHGraph.
  • ω_t: Its attention weight in the sentence.

Why attention? Terms have different effects on sentence meaning. Attention weighting adjusts their contributions dynamically according to context.

For false vagueness, the explained term's score is first recalculated:

Vag(t) = (f(t) + Σ f(explaining terms)) / (|explaining terms| + 1)

The adjusted value then enters attention-weighted sentence scoring. More specific explanations lower the term's effective score, directly reflecting contextual clarification in the final result.

4.3 Calculating Vagueness of Privacy Policies

For a policy p, VASH averages the scores of sentences whose vagueness is greater than 0 to obtain the final Vag(p).

The evaluation has two parts:

  1. Comparative experiments: Test semantic relationship prediction, vagueness-related term identification, and false vagueness detection.
  2. Survey-based evaluation: Use 44 human participants and 3 LLMs to assess agreement with human judgments.

1 Evaluation datasets and metrics

Comparative experiments use three datasets:

  • Semantic Relationship Prediction: 1,000 randomly sampled candidate term pairs.
  • Vagueness-related Term Identification: PriPolicy.
  • False Vagueness Detection: 200 sentences from PriPolicy, half flagged by SHGraph, OPI, or PolicyLint.

The first and third test sets are independently reviewed by 3 annotators, with disagreements resolved by majority voting.

Metrics are:

  • Relationship prediction: Precision, Recall, and F1.
  • Term identification: Average terms detected per policy, term_avg.
  • False vagueness: Precision, Recall, F1, and average explained sentences per policy, exsent_avg.

Because no existing approach matches VASH's entire objective, baselines are selected by subtask:

  • Relationship prediction: A CNN-based relation prediction approach for privacy policy analysis.
  • Term identification: DPV, PolicyLint, and OPI.
  • False vagueness detection: PolicyLint, OPI, and the method of Lian et al.

2 Q1: Is semantic relationship prediction accurate?

VASH achieves an overall F1 of 0.80 for semantic relationship prediction.

GPT-4o performs consistently across hypernym–hyponym and unrelated pairs, with Precision > 0.75 and Recall > 0.77. The CNN baseline struggles with subtle semantic boundaries, achieving only 0.47 Recall on hypernym–hyponym relation identification.

This validates the graph-construction combination of candidate pairs, retrieved policy context, and LLM relationship judgments. Accurate relationships underpin SHGraph's hierarchy, term scores, and false vagueness detection.

3 Q2: Does SHGraph cover more genuinely vagueness-related terms?

SHGraph identifies 100.21 vagueness-related terms per policy on average, substantially more than DPV and PolicyLint and close to OPI's 111.92.

A larger count is not inherently better. OPI includes broad privacy vocabulary such as privacy policy, which is not necessarily related to vagueness. SHGraph targets terms that are actually relevant to vagueness, rather than simply enlarging the vocabulary.

4 Q3: Can false vagueness be detected accurately?

Results below list exsent_avg, Precision, Recall, and F1, respectively:

  • PolicyLint: 3.88 / 0.69 / 0.58 / 0.63.
  • OPI: 4.30 / 0.72 / 0.37 / 0.49.
  • SHGraph: 7.84 / 0.74 / 0.92 / 0.82.

SHGraph finds 7.84 explained sentences per policy, approximately twice as many as PolicyLint and OPI. Compared with Lian et al.'s Precision 0.71, Recall 0.68, and F1 0.69, VASH achieves 0.74 / 0.92 / 0.82. The largest gain is in Recall, indicating that it identifies more existing explanations.

VASH then recalibrates explained terms' scores, carrying those results into sentence- and document-level assessment.

5 Q4: Do the identified terms affect human judgments of vagueness?

The survey includes 44 human participants and three LLMs: GPT-4o, GPT-4-turbo, and Claude-3.5-Sonnet. All complete the same questionnaire. Task 1 presents 10 real policy sentences with VASH-identified terms highlighted, asking participants to:

  1. Indicate whether the highlighted terms affect their vagueness judgments.
  2. Rate each sentence's vagueness on a 5-point Likert scale.

86.36% of human participants agree that the identified terms affect their judgments, with LLM responses providing further support.

The paper divides VASH's 0–100 scores by 20 to map them to 0–5 for comparison with Likert ratings. Overall trends agree well with human and LLM ratings.

Sentence 8 is a notable example:

we will not sell or rent your personal data to third parties for marketing purposes.

Participants generally view it as clear or neutral, but VASH assigns relatively high vagueness. Legal experts reviewing the case consider VASH's judgment reasonable because key terms such as personal data and marketing purposes lack specific definitions. This illustrates sensitivity to sentences that read naturally but retain broad key concepts.

6 Q5: Does relative sentence vagueness agree with human judgments?

Task 2 presents 10 comparable sentence pairs and asks whether A or B is more vague, or whether the participant is uncertain.

VASH generally agrees strongly with majority human and LLM judgments. The main differences occur in T2.5 and T2.7. In T2.5, 65.91% of humans select Sentence A, agreeing with VASH. GPT-4o and Claude-3.5-Sonnet select B because its data collection scope is broader, while GPT-4-turbo selects Uncertain.

In T2.7, human choices are nearly evenly split, and Claude-3.5-Sonnet also selects Uncertain. VASH assigns 93.91 and 99.63, a similarly small gap. Its numerical scores thus reflect cases where the relative difference is small, in addition to providing a ranking.

After validating VASH, the paper applies it to PriPolicy across time, company size, and industry, yielding four findings.

Finding 1: Policies became longer and more explanatory over the past decade, with little improvement in clarity

Between 2016 and 2025:

  • Average policy length rises from 60.06 to 67.42.
  • Average explained sentences rise from 6.74 to 7.69.
  • Average vagueness falls only from 82.13 to 81.99, an improvement of 0.14.

Updates change noticeably in 2019. The proportion of companies adding explained sentences is twice that in 2017 and 1.24 times that in 2018. The proportion lengthening policies is 1.45 times that in 2017 and 1.14 times that in 2018. This may be related to GDPR taking effect in 2018.

After 2020, update indicators such as %_leninc and %_exsentinc decline, suggesting limited motivation for sustained clarity improvements as regulatory pressure weakens.

Finding 2: Company size has no significant effect on vagueness, but larger companies provide more explanations

Vagueness does not differ significantly across company sizes, although larger companies have longer policies with more explanatory sentences. The paper suggests that complex operations can encourage broader wording in large companies, while smaller companies' narrower business scope makes specificity easier. Large companies also tend to provide additional clarification.

Finding 3: Industry significantly affects vagueness and explanatory behavior

Industry differences are significant. Oil, Gas, and Mining has the highest average vagueness and lowest variance, while Hospitals and Health Care has the lowest vagueness, possibly reflecting stricter regulation of sensitive health data.

Technology, Information, and Media averages 79.40 sentences and 10.20 explained sentences, exceeding traditional industries. These differences may reflect variation in regulatory attention.

Finding 4: Updating a policy does not necessarily improve clarity

Only approximately 50% of policy updates reduce vagueness. Outside 2018–2020:

  • Fewer than 30% of updates add explained sentences.
  • Only approximately 43% increase policy length.

Longer or updated policies are not necessarily clearer. What matters is whether additions provide effective, specific explanations: quality over quantity.
